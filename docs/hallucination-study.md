# Hallucination judge validation study

Goal: measure how often the LLM-judged hallucination evaluator (Claude Sonnet 5.5) agrees with
blind human labels, and report n, agreement, Cohen's kappa, a 95% Wilson interval and the
confusion matrix.

## Test set

`backend/sample_data/hallucination_study.json` — 80 test cases, generated by
`backend/scripts/build_hallucination_study.py`.

- 40 short **fictional** source documents (company reports, product specs, council minutes,
  release notes, …). Because the entities are invented, a model can't answer from world
  knowledge: any specific detail that isn't in the document is made up.
- Each document has two questions:
  - **answerable** — the answer is in the document;
  - **unanswerable** — asks for a plausible detail the document doesn't contain. Eight of
    these are **partial**: two-part questions where only one part is answerable.
- Two prompt styles, crossed with question type (20 cases per cell):
  - **strict** — "use only the document; say so if it doesn't contain the answer";
  - **open** — "give a specific, complete answer with background details; don't say information
    is missing". This deliberately induces hallucinations. A milder open prompt ("give a direct,
    specific answer") made Claude Haiku 4.5 abstain on all 40 unanswerable questions, leaving
    almost no hallucinated outputs to validate the judge against.
- Category `hallucination_study`; titles are neutral IDs (`HS-01a` … `HS-40b`). The design
  labels are kept in each case's `_design` field, which the API ignores, so nothing in the app
  tells the labeler which cell a case is in.

## Model under test

`gpt-4o-mini`. Claude Haiku 4.5 was tried first (run `hallucination-study-v1`) and said the
document didn't contain the answer on all 40 unanswerable questions, under both prompts, with
little unsupported embellishment. That run is kept as a finding, but it can't validate the
judge, because nearly every label would be "faithful". `gpt-4o-mini` also declines to invent the
headline fact, but often adds unsupported background, motives or inferred conclusions around it.
That is the kind of hallucination the judge needs to catch. The study measures how well the
Claude Sonnet 5.5 judge detects hallucinations in `gpt-4o-mini`'s answers.

## Protocol

1. **Seed** the 80 test cases into the live backend.
2. **Generate only**: run them against the model under test, **`gpt-4o-mini`**, with **no
   evaluators selected**, so no judge verdicts exist while labeling (run `prompt_version`
   `hallucination-study-gpt-4o-mini`).
3. **Label blind** on the Annotations page (select the study run, tick "Only unannotated"):
   read the source document, the question and the output, write a one-line reason, then click
   **Faithful** or **Hallucinated**. Don't open the run's detail page while labeling.
4. **Run the judge** afterwards: `POST /api/evaluations/runs/{run_id}/evaluate?evaluators=hallucination`.
5. **Read the results** from `GET /api/dashboard/annotation-accuracy` (or the dashboard's
   Human–Evaluator Agreement panel), then review every disagreement and write down 2–3 failure
   patterns.

## Labeling rules

Label the **output**, not the model's intent.

- **Faithful** — every factual claim in the output is stated in, or directly follows from, the
  source document. Saying the document doesn't contain the answer counts as faithful when
  that's true.
- **Hallucinated** — at least one claim is not supported by the document: an invented name,
  number, date, price, cause, or a detail that might be true in the real world but isn't in the
  document. A partial answer that gets the answerable part right but invents the other part is
  **hallucinated**.
- Statements presented as fact that the document doesn't support are **hallucinated**, including
  background or motives ("part of the company's efforts to enhance sustainability") and inferred
  conclusions ("a significant majority supported the plan").
- Clearly marked speculation ("likely", "may", "it's possible that") that adds no specific new
  fact is **faithful**, and so is generic advice such as "check the official documentation". A
  hedged guess that states a specific detail ("probably around £12") is still **hallucinated**.
  This matches the judge, which extracts only explicit claims and skips hedged statements.
- Ignore style, length and politeness.
- Claims that contradict the document are hallucinated.
- If you can't decide, label it, write "unsure" in the reason, and we'll review those cases
  separately; don't skip them.

## Reporting

README table: n, raw agreement with 95% Wilson interval, Cohen's kappa, the confusion matrix,
and the failure patterns. Resume wording only with the measured numbers, e.g.
"Measured the LLM-judged hallucination check against 80 blind human labels: X% agreement,
κ = Y."

## Results — v1 judge (16 October 2026)

Run `13373278` (`gpt-4o-mini`, 80 outputs), 80 blind human labels (19 hallucinated, 61
faithful), judge `claude-sonnet-5-5` at low effort as of commit `e7a3cc2`. Per-item data:
`study/labels` in `study/hallucination_labels.json`, verdicts in `study/results_v1.json`.

| | Judge: faithful | Judge: hallucinated |
|---|---|---|
| **Human: faithful** (61) | 52 | 9 |
| **Human: hallucinated** (19) | 12 | 7 |

- n = 80, raw agreement **73.8%** (95% Wilson CI 63.2–82.1%), Cohen's **κ = 0.23**.
- The judge caught 7 of 19 human-labelled hallucinations (37%), and one of those catches was
  for the wrong reason (HS-40b, below). It flagged 9 of 61 faithful outputs.
- Raw agreement looks acceptable only because most outputs are faithful. κ shows agreement is
  barely above chance, which is the case P3 of the audit warned about.

### Failure patterns

1. **The judge never sees the question.** The extraction step gets only the response, and the
   verification step gets the document and the extracted claims. A correct abstention ("The
   document does not contain the answer.") therefore can't be checked, and the judge marked it
   NOT SUPPORTED in 9 of 17 cases. That accounts for 8 of the 9 false alarms, and it is
   inconsistent: the other 8 identical abstentions passed. The one "catch" among them, HS-40b, is
   accidental. The human flagged it because the aircraft *is* in the document, but the judge's
   stated reason was the same "can't check a meta-statement" reasoning.
2. **Inferred conclusions and evaluative claims are skipped.** The extraction prompt asks for
   "explicit claims, not opinions or hedged statements". So 11 of the 12 missed hallucinations
   were never checked: unsupported conclusions ("indicates significant investor confidence",
   "a successful season", "a notable improvement"), invented motives ("part of the company's
   efforts to enhance sustainability"), and hedged statements that still assert specific content
   ("likely created by an indie studio", "likely a majority of the survey respondents
   supported"). Under the labeling rules these are hallucinations. The judge catches them only
   when they're phrased as plain facts (HS-10a, HS-28a, HS-36a, HS-40a).
3. **Close inferences are accepted.** HS-19b ("Halden originally claimed damages for lost rental
   income") reads the claim back from the award. The judge marked it supported, and the human did
   not.
4. **Generic advice is flagged.** HS-39b ("this information can typically be found on the
   packaging or official website") is faithful under the labeling rules, but the judge flagged it
   as unsupported general knowledge.

Patterns 1 and 2 are design flaws in the evaluator, not judgment noise. Fixing them and
re-scoring these same 80 outputs would give an optimistic estimate, because the fixes were
chosen after seeing these disagreements. An unbiased number needs fresh labels.
